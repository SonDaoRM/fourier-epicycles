1. The big idea

Take one circle spinning at some speed. Put a smaller circle on its rim, spinning at a different speed. Put a third on that one. The tip of the last arm traces a path. Joseph Fourier's claim, in modern dress, is wild: any closed curve, a heart, a star, your signature, is the path of such a chain of circles, if you pick the sizes and speeds right.

z(t) = Σn cn eint,   cn = (1/M) Σk z(tk) e−intk

Each coefficient cn is one circle: its length is |cn|, its starting angle is the phase of cn, and its spin rate is n turns per trip around the drawing (negative n spins backwards). The machine below computes these circles for real curves, live, using the formula above.

2. The epicycle machine

Pick a shape: heart, star, square, infinity, the outline of RMIT's red pixel logo, or a mystery face drawn from one continuous pen path (guess who?). The pen is the tip of the last circle. Start with 1 circle (just an ellipse-ish wobble), then slide up and watch the machine learn the shape. Try the square or the RMIT mark: circles hate corners, and you can see why.

Which circles matter?

Circle sizes, biggest first. The lit bars are the ones your slider is currently using. For the heart, a handful of big circles does almost all the work; that is why MP3 and JPEG compression work at all.

What the heart is made of

x = 16 sin³(t)
y = 13 cos(t) − 5 cos(2t) − 2 cos(3t) − cos(4t)

The heart from the poster. It is already a tiny Fourier series, only 1 sine and 4 cosine terms, so about 9 circles redraw it almost perfectly. Slide to 9 circles on the heart and watch the error collapse.

3. Waves: the classic Fourier series

The same trick works for ordinary waves y(t). Here each circle adds one sine term. The chain on the left spins; the pen's height is the wave's value, unrolled to the right as time passes. Add terms and watch a stack of smooth sines grow corners.

partial sum (your terms) target wave

square: y(t) = (4/π) Σk sin((2k+1)t) / (2k+1)

Gibbs phenomenon. Crank the terms to 25 on the square wave. The corners sharpen, but the little ear next to each jump never dies: it stays about 9% of the jump height, no matter how many circles you add. It only gets squeezed thinner. Smooth waves (triangle) converge nicely; jumps never fully forgive you.

4. A real machine that does this

Kids have played with a mechanical cousin of this machine for decades: the Spirograph, a gear rolling inside a ring. One gear spinning inside another is exactly two circles chained together, and its pen draws hypotrochoids, the simplest epicycle curves. Our machine is the same idea with the training wheels off: as many circles as you like, each with its own size, speed and direction.

The math that finds the circles, the Fourier transform, runs inside every phone call (MP3/AAC audio), every photo (JPEG), every MRI scan, and every noise-cancelling headphone.

5. Model honesty: what this demo assumes

  • Sampling. The machine does not see the true mathematical curve. It sees M = 2048 equally spaced points on each shape and runs a discrete Fourier transform, so even at the slider's top it reproduces the sampled curve, not the ideal one.
  • Finite terms. A true Fourier series can need infinitely many circles. The slider caps at 2000 circles (raised from 500 in this version). All 2000 coefficients contribute to the pen position, so corners and fine detail are always approximate. The error number under the machine is the RMS distance to the 2048-point sampled target, computed from the omitted coefficients (Parseval's identity), not a pixel comparison with the scan. The trace is drawn from 16,384 points of the selected Fourier sum, so fine detail is not skipped between animation frames. FFT calculation, cached shapes and an incremental trace keep high counts responsive. All selected terms contribute to the pen position; circle outlines and arms smaller than half a canvas pixel are not painted, because they would only overdraw the same pixels. Speed still depends on your device.
  • Gibbs overshoot. For discontinuous targets (square wave, the square's corners), partial sums overshoot by about 9% of the jump. More terms shrink the overshoot's width, not its height. That is a theorem, not a bug in this page.
  • Order of circles. Circles are drawn biggest-first for clarity. Physically the order along the chain does not change the final path, only the intermediate wiggles.
  • Music. Each shape has its own synthesized tune - heart, star, square, infinity, the RMIT mark and the Guess who? face each play a different chord progression and melody, generated note-by-note in this page (Web Audio oscillators, no recordings, works offline). Music is on by default per request; browsers and phones block all sound until you touch the page, so playback begins on your first tap anywhere, and the button then simply toggles it. The tunes do not affect the math.
  • RMIT logo. The "RMIT logo" shape traces the outer outline of RMIT University's red pixel mark, sampled directly from the vector file RMIT University Logo.svg (Wikimedia Commons; the same mark design is served on rmit.edu.au today). The outline's 11 straight steps and 2 curved arcs are taken from that file's path data as-is, so the silhouette is geometrically faithful to the source vector; the 2048-point resampling adds only a sub-pixel blur. Only the outer silhouette is traced - the "RMIT UNIVERSITY" wordmark and the red fill are not part of the drawing, and the mark itself remains RMIT's trademark.
  • Guess who? The "Guess who?" shape traces a hand-drawn line drawing of a face supplied by the page's author - head outline, scribbled hair, brows, eyes, nose, lips, chin, ear and neck. It traces the drawn lines themselves, not a silhouette. The scanned strokes were thinned to one-pixel skeletons and ordered into a single continuous pen path (about 13,900 px of ink in the scan, simplified to 1,685 vertices, then sampled at M = 2048 for this shape). The pen never lifts: the drawing's separate strokes are joined by about 220 straight connecting moves, most of them shorter than 3 px on the roughly 370 px drawing and effectively invisible, plus a few longer chords (five over 25 px, the longest 48 px) that are joins, not original lines. Faithfulness to the traced lines: RMS error 23.6 px with 10 circles, 14.3 px at 25, 8.2 px at 50, 4.3 px at 100, 2.1 px at 200 and 0.8 px at 500 - the face is unmistakable by 50 circles and near-exact by 500. That is why this drawing needs more circles than a heart or a logo outline. The slider now runs to 2000; at that setting its sampled-path RMS error is about 0.0103 px. More circles do not remove the connecting chords, restore ink lost during tracing, or make the traced path identical to the original scan.
  • Real-world claims. MP3, JPEG and MRI use Fourier's idea but with extra engineering (psychoacoustics, quantization, k-space physics). The circle picture is the honest core, not the whole device.
